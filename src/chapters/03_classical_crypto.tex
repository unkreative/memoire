\chapter{\gls{classical-cryptography}}\label{chap:classical_crypto}

\gls{classical-cryptography} represents the time tested methods that have protected sensitive information long before quantum computing became a concern. In this chapter, I examine these established techniques, exploring their origins, the mathematical principles they rely on, and the practical algorithms in use today. By doing so, I prepare the ground for Chapter~\ref{chap:quantum_impact}, where I assess how quantum advances undermine these very foundations.

\section{Historical Development of Cryptography}
Cryptography has evolved over millennia in response to military, diplomatic, and commercial needs for secrecy \parencite{kahn1996codebreakers, singh1999code}. Its origins trace back to simple \gls{transposition-cipher} devices like the Spartan scytale (circa 400\,BCE) and early symbol \glspl{substitution-cipher} \parencite{madness2020classical}.

During the \textbf{Classical Period}, \gls{monoalphabetic-cipher} ciphers such as Caesar gave way to \gls{polyalphabetic-cipher} schemes like Vigen\`ere to frustrate \gls{frequency-analysis}; later, \gls{digraph-cipher} ciphers such as Playfair and linear algebraic approaches like the \gls{hill-cipher} added further complexity \parencite{stallings2017cryptography}.

The \textbf{Mechanical Era} introduced machines like \gls{enigma} that automated encryption using rotating wheels (Figure~\ref{fig:enigma_rotors_ch3}). Cracking Enigma helped drive early computer development. The \textbf{Modern Era} began with Claude Shannon's work in 1949 \parencite{shannon1949communication}, leading to standards like \gls{des} in 1977 \parencite{nist1999des}. This era established that security should rely on secret keys rather than secret methods, and brought \gls{public-key-cryptography} in 1976 \parencite{diffie1976new}, transforming how keys are shared securely.

\section{Fundamentals of Modern Cryptography}\label{sec:crypto_fundamentals_ch3_rev}\label{sec:security_foundations_ch3}
Modern cryptography assumes adversaries possess only classical computing resources. Security guarantees derive from well studied mathematical problems.

\subsection{Core Security Goals}
Cryptosystems aim to provide \textbf{\gls{confidentiality}}, \textbf{\gls{integrity}}, \textbf{\gls{authentication}}, and \textbf{\gls{non-repudiation}} through combinations of encryption, hashing, \glspl{mac}, and \glspl{digital-signature} \parencite{katz2014introduction, stallings2017cryptography}.

\subsection{Computational Hardness Assumptions}\label{subsec:hardness_assumptions_ch3_rev}
Key primitives rely on problems believed intractable for classical machines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Integer Factorization (IFP)}: Hard to factor \(N = p q\), the basis of RSA.
  \item \textbf{Discrete Logarithm (DLP)}: Hard to find \(x\) from \(g^x = h\) in a finite group, underpinning Diffie Hellman and DSA.
  \item \textbf{Elliptic Curve DLP (ECDLP)}: Hard to find scalar \(k\) from \(Q = kP\) on an elliptic curve, enabling \gls{ecc}.
\end{itemize}
These support \gls{one-way-function} and \gls{trapdoor-function} functions whose classical difficulty will be reexamined under quantum attacks.

\section{\gls{symmetric-key-cryptography}}\label{sec:symmetric_key_ch3_rev}\label{sec:symmetric_key_ch3}
Symmetric algorithms use a shared secret key for encryption and decryption. They excel at high speed bulk encryption but require secure key distribution \parencite{katz2014introduction}.

\subsection{Design and Security}
\glspl{block-cipher} like \gls{aes} combine substitution (\gls{confusion}) and permutation (\gls{diffusion}) across multiple rounds, with a complex \gls{key-schedule}. A key of \(n\) bits yields a search space of \(2^n\), giving \(n\) bit classical security (Equation~\ref{eq:key_space_ch3_rev}). Implementations must also resist \glspl{side-channel-attack} such as \gls{timing-attack} and \gls{power-analysis} to realize this security in practice \parencite{kocher1996timing, mangard2007power}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:key_space_ch3_rev}
    \text{Security level (bits)} = n = \log_2(2^n)
\end{equation}
\newpage

\subsection{Modes of Operation}
To encrypt long messages, \glspl{block-cipher} use \glspl{mode-of-operation} such as \gls{cbc} and \gls{gc}M \parencite{dworkin, nist_sp800_38d}. CBC hides patterns across blocks. GCM adds integrity and authentication. \gls{ecb} is insecure since identical plaintext blocks yield identical ciphertext (Figure~\ref{fig:ecb_vs_cbc_rev}).

\section{\gls{public-key-cryptography}}\label{sec:public_key_ch3_rev}\label{sec:public_key_ch3}
Asymmetric cryptography solves key distribution by using a public private key pair. Its security depends on \gls{ifp-dlp} type assumptions that quantum algorithms will break.

\subsection{\gls{rsa}}\label{subsec:rsa_ch3_rev}\label{subsec:rsa_ch3}
RSA publishes \((n, e)\) and keeps \((n, d)\) secret with \(e d \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}\).  
Encryption \(C = M^e \bmod n\).  
Decryption \(M = C^d \bmod n\).  
Recommended sizes 2048 to 3072 bits \parencite{nist_sp800_57p1r5}. Padding schemes such as \gls{oaep} prevent adaptive attacks \parencite{bleichenbacher1998chosen}. \gls{shors-algorithm} breaks RSA by factoring \(n\) efficiently.

\subsection{\gls{diffie-hellman} Key Exchange}\label{subsec:dh_ch3_rev}
DH enables two parties to agree on a shared secret \(S = g^{ab} \bmod p\) without prior secrets \parencite{diffie1976new}. Public values \(p, g, A = g^a, B = g^b\). Shared secret \(S = B^a = A^b\). \gls{shors-algorithm} recovers \(a, b\), breaking DH.
\newpage

\subsection{\gls{ecc} (Elliptic Curve Cryptography)}\label{subsec:ecc_ch3_rev}\label{subsec:ecc_ch3}
ECC achieves equivalent security to RSA with much smaller keys (a 256 bit ECC key is roughly equivalent to a 3072 bit RSA key) \parencite{nist_sp800_57p1r5}. It relies on the hardness of \gls{ecdlp}. \gls{shors-algorithm} also breaks ECC by solving ECDLP efficiently.

\section{\glspl{hash-function}}\label{sec:hash_functions_ch3_rev}\label{sec:hash_functions_ch3}
Cryptographic hashes map any input to a fixed size digest. They ensure integrity and underpin signatures and MACs \parencite{menezes_hash}.

\subsection{Security Requirements}
A secure cryptographic \gls{hash-function} must satisfy several key properties. \textbf{\gls{preimage-resistance}} ensures that given a hash output \(h\), it is computationally infeasible to find any input message \(m\) such that \(H(m) = h\). \textbf{\gls{second-preimage-resistance}} means that given an input \(m_1\), it is computationally infeasible to find a distinct input \(m_2 \neq m_1\) such that \(H(m_1) = H(m_2)\). Finally, \textbf{\gls{collision-resistance}} dictates that it must be computationally infeasible to find any pair of distinct inputs \(m_1, m_2\) where \(m_1 \neq m_2\) but \(H(m_1) = H(m_2)\). While older standards like \gls{md5} and \gls{sha-1} have been demonstrably broken with respect to collision resistance \parencite{wang2005finding}, modern standards such as \gls{sha-2} and \gls{sha-3} are designed to uphold these properties and remain secure against known classical attacks \parencite{nist_fips202, blake3}.

\section{\glspl{digital-signature} and \gls{pki}}\label{sec:digital_signatures_pki_ch3_rev}\label{sec:digital_signatures_pki_ch3}
\glspl{digital-signature} combine hashes with asymmetric keys to provide \gls{authentication} integrity and \gls{non-repudiation} \parencite{menezes_signatures}.

\subsection{Signing and Verification}
The Signer computes \(H(M)\) then \(S = \mathrm{Sign}_{K_{\mathrm{priv}}}(H(M))\), and the Verifier checks \(\mathrm{Verify}_{K_{\mathrm{pub}}}(S) \stackrel{?}{=} H(M)\).

\subsection{PKI Trust Model}
Certificate Authorities vouch for public keys enabling trust in TLS and HTTPS (Figure~\ref{fig:pki_model_ch3_rev}) \parencite{stallings2017cryptography}.

\section{Protocol and Implementation Security}\label{sec:protocol_design_ch3_rev}\label{sec:protocol_design_ch3}
Strong algorithms alone do not guarantee security. Protocol design and implementation rigor are equally vital \parencite{boneh2020graduate}.

\subsection{Protocol Vulnerabilities}
Man in the Middle replay and downgrade attacks arise when authentication or freshness checks are missing.

\subsection{Implementation Vulnerabilities}
Side channel leaks such as timing power and electromagnetic analysis padding oracles memory errors and poor randomness can all break otherwise secure schemes.

\section{Conclusion: The Classical Security Model and Its Limits}\label{sec:classical_limits_ch3_rev}
This chapter surveyed how classical cryptography safeguards information using symmetric ciphers public key mechanisms cryptographic hashes and trust infrastructures all grounded in problems believed to resist classical computation. However these foundations become fragile under quantum scrutiny. Algorithms such as Shor's and Grover's expose their vulnerabilities. Understanding these limitations is critical before turning to quantum resilient alternatives in Chapter~\ref{chap:quantum_resistant}.
