\section{Shor's Algorithm: Breaking Public-Key Cryptography}\label{sec:shors_impact_ch5_rev}

In 1994, Peter Shor introduced an algorithm that fundamentally altered the landscape of cryptographic security \parencite{shor1994algorithms}. \Gls{shors-algorithm} represents the single most significant quantum threat to modern cryptography by achieving an exponential speedup over classical algorithms for two foundational problems: the \gls{integer-factorization-problem} and the \gls{discrete-logarithm-problem}. This revolutionary capability emerges from its elegant application of quantum phenomena, particularly the \gls{qft} (Section~\ref{subsec:qft}) and \gls{qpe} (Section~\ref{subsec:qpe}).

\subsection{The Quantum Advantage in Integer Factorization}\label{subsec:shors}

The security of much modern digital infrastructure relies on computational problems believed intractable for classical computers. The \gls{integer-factorization-problem}—finding the prime factors of large composite numbers—stands as the cornerstone of \gls{rsa} cryptography. While the best classical algorithms require sub-exponential time (like the \gls{gnfs}), Shor's algorithm achieves factorization in polynomial time, dramatically reducing the resources required, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:shor-vs-classical-complexity_ch5_rev}.

The algorithm's core strength lies in transforming the factorization problem into a period-finding problem. By preparing a quantum superposition, applying modular exponentiation, and using the \gls{qft}, Shor's algorithm efficiently discovers the period of this function, which then allows efficient calculation of the factors via classical methods.

\subsection{Cryptographic Systems Under Threat}\label{subsec:shor_impact_specific_ch5_rev}

The exponential speedup provided by Shor's algorithm directly undermines the security assumptions of virtually all widely deployed public-key cryptosystems \parencite{bernstein2017post}:

\begin{itemize} % Using itemize for better readability
    \item \textbf{\gls{rsa} Encryption and Signatures:} The security of \gls{rsa} (Section~\ref{subsec:rsa_ch3_rev}) relies entirely on the difficulty of factoring the public modulus $N$. Shor's algorithm makes factoring $N$ feasible, allowing an attacker with a \gls{crqc} to derive the private key $d$ from the public key $(N,e)$, breaking the system completely, regardless of key length.
    \item \textbf{\gls{dh} and \gls{dsa}:} These protocols (Section~\ref{subsec:dh_ch3_rev}) depend on the classical hardness of the \gls{discrete-logarithm-problem} (DLP) in finite fields. Shor's algorithm provides an efficient quantum method to solve the DLP, rendering \gls{dh} and the related \gls{dsa} insecure.
    \item \textbf{Elliptic Curve Cryptography (\gls{ecc}):} \Gls{ecc} systems like \gls{ecdh} and \gls{ecdsa} (Section~\ref{subsec:ecc_ch3_rev}) rely on the hardness of the \gls{ecdlp}. Shor's algorithm, adapted for elliptic curve groups, also solves \gls{ecdlp} efficiently, compromising the security of these compact and widely used schemes.
\end{itemize}
\medskip % Adds a bit of vertical space between items

The security implications extend beyond theoretical concerns. Current resource estimates, while substantial (e.g., millions of physical qubits for RSA-2048 \parencite{gidney2021factor}), establish a clear trajectory where these cryptosystems become insecure, driving the need for alternatives.

\subsection{Broader Implications for Digital Security}

The threat from Shor's algorithm cascades through the entire digital ecosystem. Core internet security protocols, including \gls{tls} (securing HTTPS), \gls{ssh} (secure remote access), \gls{ipsec} (VPNs), and the \gls{pki} infrastructure built upon these algorithms, become vulnerable.

Unlike classical vulnerabilities often mitigated by patches or larger keys, the quantum threat is fundamental. No increase in \gls{rsa} or \gls{ecc} key size offers long-term protection against Shor's algorithm. This necessitates the development and deployment of fundamentally different, quantum-resistant cryptographic approaches.

While Shor's algorithm poses an existential threat to public-key cryptography, another quantum algorithm, Grover's, presents a different kind of challenge to symmetric primitives.

\section{Grover's Algorithm: Weakening Symmetric Cryptography and Hashes}\label{sec:grovers_impact_ch5_rev}

In contrast to Shor's exponential speedup for specific problems, \gls{grovers-algorithm} \parencite{grover1996fast} offers a more general *quadratic* speedup for \gls{unstructured-search} problems. This impacts cryptographic primitives whose security relies partly on the difficulty of exhaustive search: symmetric-key ciphers and \gls{hash-function,plural}. It operates by amplifying the probability amplitude of target states within a quantum superposition (see Section~\ref{subsec:amplitude_amp_ch2_revised}).

\subsection{Mechanism: Quantum Amplitude Amplification}\label{subsec:grover_mechanism_ch5_rev}
Grover's algorithm initializes a system into an equal superposition of all $N$ possible states in a search space. It then iteratively applies an "\gls{grover-oracle}" $U_f$ that marks target state(s) (e.g., by phase inversion) and a "\gls{grover-diffusion}" operator $U_s$ that amplifies the amplitude of marked states while reducing others (performing \gls{amplitude-amplification}). This process, shown conceptually in Figure~\ref{fig:grover_circuit_ch5_rev}, finds a target state with high probability in about $O(\sqrt{N})$ quantum queries, compared to $O(N)$ classical queries for \gls{unstructured-search} \parencite{nielsen_chuang_book}.

\subsection{Impact on Symmetric Keys and Hash Functions}\label{subsec:grover_impact_specific_ch5_rev}
% Keeping sloppypar for now, but recommend rephrasing during final edits if possible
\begin{sloppypar}
The quadratic speedup impacts the effective security of symmetric primitives \parencite{bernstein2017post}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symmetric Key Search:} Recovering an $n$-bit symmetric key (e.g., for \gls{aes}, Section~\ref{sec:symmetric_key_ch3_rev}) classically takes $O(2^n)$ operations via brute force. Grover's reduces the quantum complexity to $O(\sqrt{2^n}) = O(2^{n/2})$ operations. This effectively halves the bit security against quantum brute-force search. For example, AES-128 (128-bit classical security) offers only about $128/2 = 64$ bits of security against Grover's algorithm.
    \item \textbf{Hash Function Pre-images:} Finding an input $x$ for a target hash output $y$ (i.e., $H(x)=y$, Section~\ref{sec:hash_functions_ch3_rev}) is an \gls{unstructured-search}. For an ideal $n$-bit hash, Grover's reduces the complexity of a \gls{preimage-attack} from classical $O(2^n)$ to quantum $O(2^{n/2})$.
    \item \textbf{Hash Function Collisions:} Finding distinct inputs $x_1 \neq x_2$ where $H(x_1)=H(x_2)$ (a \gls{collision-attack}) classically uses the \gls{birthday-attack} ($O(2^{n/2})$ complexity). Quantum algorithms improve on this, potentially reaching $O(2^{n/3})$ complexity in some models \parencite{bernstein2017post}, although the practical advantage over the classical birthday bound is still debated for standard hashes. The primary defense remains using a hash function with a large enough output size ($n$) such that $n/2$ provides sufficient collision resistance.
\end{itemize}
Crucially, Grover's algorithm weakens these primitives but does not break them exponentially like Shor does for public-key systems. The standard mitigation is to increase key or hash output sizes. For instance, using AES-256 provides approximately 128 bits of quantum security against key search. Using SHA-256 or SHA-384 provides 128 or 192 bits of quantum security against \gls{preimage-attack,plural}, respectively, and their collision resistance remains dominated by the classical \gls{birthday-attack} bound.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Synthesizing the Threat: Security Levels and Comparison}\label{sec:synthesis_ch5_rev}\label{sec:security_levels_ch5_rev} % Combined labels for simplicity

The differing impacts of Shor's and Grover's algorithms necessitate distinct mitigation strategies and define the tiered nature of the quantum threat.

\subsection{Required Security Level Adjustments}\label{subsec:security_levels_ch5_rev}
To maintain a target security level (e.g., 128 bits, corresponding roughly to \gls{nist-security-levels} 1 or 3) against both classical and quantum adversaries, parameters must be chosen carefully:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shor-Vulnerable Systems (\gls{rsa}, DH, \gls{ecc}):} Offer essentially zero security against a \gls{crqc}. They must be replaced entirely by \gls{pqc} alternatives (Chapter~\ref{chap:quantum_resistant}).
    \item \textbf{Grover-Vulnerable Systems (Symmetric Ciphers, Hashes):} To achieve $k$ bits of quantum security against brute-force key search or \gls{preimage-attack,plural}, the key or hash output size generally needs to be at least $2k$ bits.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item For 128-bit quantum security (key search) $\implies$ Use \gls{aes}-256.
            \item For 128-bit quantum security (hash pre-image) $\implies$ Use SHA-256 or larger.
            \item For \gls{collision-resistance} (dominated by classical $O(2^{n/2})$ \gls{birthday-attack} bound), SHA-256 (providing 128-bit collision resistance) is often deemed sufficient, though larger hashes offer more margin.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
This leads to the practical rules: for public-key crypto, Quantum Security $\approx 0$; for symmetric/hash crypto, Quantum Security $\approx$ Classical Security / 2 (for search-based attacks).

\section{Timeline and the Urgency of Transition}\label{sec:timeline_sndl_ch5_rev}

Predicting the arrival of a \gls{crqc} capable of breaking current public-key cryptography is uncertain, yet the risk necessitates proactive migration.

\subsection{Estimates and Uncertainties}\label{subsec:timeline_ch5_rev}
Expert estimates for a CRQC often range from 10 to 20+ years, but significant uncertainty exists \parencite{mosca2018cybersecurity, nist_pqc_timeline_2024}. Hardware challenges remain, particularly achieving fault-tolerance via \gls{qec} at the scale needed for Shor's algorithm against large keys \parencite{preskill2018quantum}. Progress continues, but the timeline is difficult to pinpoint, making preparation essential regardless of the exact date.

\newpage

\subsection{The "Store Now, Decrypt Later" (SNDL) Threat}\label{subsec:sndl_ch5_rev}
The \gls{sndl} attack vector creates immediate urgency. Adversaries can capture encrypted data transmitted today (e.g., via \gls{tls}) and store it. Once a \gls{crqc} is available, this data, if still sensitive, can be decrypted retrospectively. This threat is independent of when the \gls{crqc} arrives; it depends on the required confidentiality lifetime of the data.

\Gls{moscas-inequality} highlights this urgency:
\begin{equation}
    X + Y > Z
\end{equation}
where $X$ = Security Shelf Life of data, $Y$ = Migration Time to PQC, $Z$ = Time until CRQC exists. If $X+Y > Z$, data encrypted today is already at risk. Since $Y$ can be 5-15 years for large organizations and $X$ can be decades, the migration ($Y$) must begin long before $Z$ arrives \parencite{mosca2018cybersecurity}.

\section{Conclusion: The Imperative for Quantum Resistance}\label{sec:conclusion_ch5_rev}

This chapter has demonstrated that quantum computing poses a concrete and potentially catastrophic threat to current cryptographic standards. \Gls{shors-algorithm} fundamentally breaks the mathematical foundations of widely used \gls{public-key-cryptography} (\gls{rsa}, \gls{dh}, \gls{ecc}), while \gls{grovers-algorithm} significantly weakens \gls{symmetric-key-cryptography} algorithms (\gls{aes}) and \gls{hash-function,plural} (SHA-2, SHA-3) by enabling faster brute-force style attacks (\gls{preimage-attack,plural}, key search). The looming \gls{sndl} threat transforms this from a future possibility into a present-day risk assessment requirement for data with long-term confidentiality needs.
