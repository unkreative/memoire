\chapter{Classical versus Quantum Computing}\label{chap:classical_vs_quantum_rev}

% Added introduction
This chapter contrasts the core principles forming the distintion between classical and quantum computing. By examining their fundamental units of information, operational models, and resulting computational power, we establish the context necessary to understand why quantum systems pose a unique and significant challenge to modern cryptography, a topic explored in detail in subsequent chapters.

\section{Fundamental Differences in Information Representation}\label{sec:differences_rev}

The most profound difference lies in the basic unit of information. Classical computers operate using \textbf{\glspl{bit}}, which adhere to binary logic, representing either a 0 or a 1 at any given time. Quantum computers, conversely, utilize \textbf{\glspl{qubit}} (quantum bits). As introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals}, a \gls{qubit} is governed by the principles of quantum mechanics and can exist in a state of \gls{superposition}, meaning it can represent a combination of both 0 and 1 simultaneously \parencite{nielsen_chuang_book}.

Mathematically, the state of a single \gls{qubit}, $|\psi\rangle$, is described as a linear combination of the basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qubit_state_ch4_rev}
    |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers known as \glspl{probability-amplitude}, satisfying the normalization condition $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. The values $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ represent the probabilities of measuring the \gls{qubit} in the state $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, respectively. This ability to exist in multiple states at once allows quantum systems to explore a vastly larger computational state space compared to classical systems of the same size.

The power of \gls{superposition} becomes exponentially more potent when considering multiple \glspl{qubit}. A classical register of $n$ \glspl{bit} can store exactly one of $2^n$ possible configurations. In stark contrast, a quantum register of $n$ \glspl{qubit} can, through \gls{superposition}, simultaneously represent all $2^n$ configurations.

% Consider reviewing this paragraph for conciseness/clarity
\begin{sloppypar}
Furthermore, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:entanglement}, \glspl{qubit} can exhibit \gls{entanglement}, a uniquely quantum correlation where the state of multiple \glspl{qubit} becomes intrinsically linked, regardless of physical separation \parencite{nielsen_chuang_book}. Measuring the state of one entangled \gls{qubit} instantaneously influences the state of the others in a way that cannot be explained by classical physics. This non-local correlation is a crucial resource for many quantum algorithms and communication protocols.

Another fundamental distinction arises from the \gls{no-cloning-theorem} in quantum mechanics, which states that it is impossible to create an identical, independent copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state \parencite{nielsen_chuang_book}. This contrasts sharply with classical information, which can be copied freely. The no-cloning principle has profound implications for quantum information processing and security. % Removed direct QKD mention here
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Computational Models and Information Processing}\label{sec:computational_models_rev}

% Added transition phrase
Building on these differences in information representation, the computational models themselves are fundamentally distinct.

\subsection{Classical Computing Model}\label{subsec:classical_rev}
Classical computation largely follows the \gls{turing-machine} model or the \gls{von-neumann-architecture}. Information is processed sequentially (or in parallel across multiple \glspl{classical-core}) using deterministic logic gates operating on \glspl{bit}. The state of an $n$-\gls{bit} classical system is described by a single binary string of length $n$. While powerful for a vast range of tasks, this model faces inherent limitations when tackling problems whose complexity grows exponentially with input size, such as factoring large numbers or simulating complex quantum systems.
\newpage

\subsection{Quantum Computing Model}\label{subsec:quantum_rev}
Quantum computation operates within the framework of quantum mechanics, typically visualized using the \gls{quantum-circuit} model. \Glspl{qubit} states evolve through the application of \glspl{quantum-gate}, which are mathematically represented by unitary transformations acting on the state vector within a complex \gls{hilbert_space}. The state of an $n$-\gls{qubit} system is described by a vector in this $2^n$-dimensional space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantum_state_ch4_rev_updated}
    |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} \alpha_i|i\rangle, \quad \text{where} \sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} |\alpha_i|^2 = 1
\end{equation}
Each $|i\rangle$ corresponds to a classical bit string (e.g., $|011\rangle$), and $\alpha_i$ is its complex amplitude.

% Added sentence explicitly linking model to principles
The quantum circuit model leverages superposition and entanglement, allowing quantum gates to simultaneously transform all $2^n$ components of the state vector. This leads to what is often termed \gls{quantum-parallelism}, where a single quantum operation effectively performs a massive parallel computation across the entire state space. However, this parallelism is not directly accessible. Upon measurement, the quantum state collapses probabilistically into just one of the basis states $|i\rangle$ according to the probability $|\alpha_i|^2$. The true power of quantum algorithms, therefore, lies not just in parallelism but in harnessing quantum \gls{interference}—carefully choreographing the unitary evolution to constructively amplify the amplitudes of desired outcomes and destructively cancel the amplitudes of undesired ones before measurement \parencite{nielsen_chuang_book}.

\section{Comparing Computational Power and Complexity}\label{sec:complexity_classes_rev}

% Added transition phrase
These distinct operational models lead to different computational capabilities, which can be formally compared using \gls{computational-complexity-theory}. This theory classifies problems based on the resources (like time or memory) required to solve them as the input size grows.

Key complexity classes relevant to this comparison include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\Gls{p-complexity} (Polynomial time):} Problems solvable by a deterministic classical computer in time polynomial in the input size. These are considered "efficiently solvable" classically.
    \item \textbf{\Gls{np-complexity} (Nondeterministic Polynomial time):} Problems for which a proposed solution can be verified efficiently by a classical computer. It is famously unknown whether P = NP.
    \item \textbf{\Gls{bpp-complexity} (Bounded-error Probabilistic Polynomial time):} Problems solvable by a probabilistic classical computer in polynomial time with a bounded error probability (e.g., error < 1/3). BPP represents problems efficiently solvable by practical randomized classical algorithms and is often considered the class of "efficiently solvable" problems in the classical world.
    \item \textbf{\Gls{bqp} (Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time):} Problems solvable by a quantum computer in polynomial time with a bounded error probability. This class captures the power of efficient quantum computation.
\end{itemize}
\parencite{arora_barak_complexity}

% Slightly rephrased relationships for clarity
The known relationships between these classes are $\textbf{P} \subseteq \textbf{BPP} \subseteq \textbf{BQP}$. The crucial insight for cryptography is that \gls{bqp} contains problems like integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem, which are essential to the security of \gls{rsa}, \gls{ecc}, and Diffie-Hellman. These problems are strongly believed *not* to be in \gls{bpp-complexity}, meaning they are considered intractable for classical computers. However, \gls{shors-algorithm} demonstrates they are efficiently solvable by quantum computers, placing them firmly within \gls{bqp} \parencite{shor1997polynomial}. (The relationship between \gls{bqp} and \gls{np-complexity} remains an open research question.)

While Shor's algorithm provides an exponential speedup for specific structured problems, \Gls{grovers-algorithm} (Section~\ref{subsec:amplitude_amp_ch2_revised}) offers a more general quadratic speedup for unstructured search problems. Classically, finding an item in an unsorted database of size $N$ takes $O(N)$ time on average. Grover's algorithm achieves this in $O(\sqrt{N})$ quantum time. While significant, this quadratic speedup typically does not move problems between major complexity classes (like placing an NP-complete problem into BQP) in the same way Shor's algorithm does for factoring. However, as discussed later, it still impacts the practical security parameters of symmetric ciphers and hash functions (Chapter~\ref{chap:quantum_impact}).
